Dependency Parsing

Learn how a sentence is described by links between words: each word depends on a head, and each link has a label such as subject or object. Read the three short explanations, try the warm-up, then complete the 10 tasks (match the column, select all that apply, multiple choice, and click the right word), which go from easy to hard.

LEARN: HEADS AND DEPENDENTS

Dependency parsing links words to words

In dependency parsing, every word (except one) depends on another word, called its head. We draw an arc from the head to the word that depends on it, the dependent, and write a label on the arc that says how they are related.

  1. Read "chased → dog (nsubj)" as: dog is the subject of chased.
  2. The ROOT is the main word, usually the main verb. It depends on nothing, and the whole sentence hangs from it.

LEARN: RELATION LABELS

The label tells you the job of each link

These are the labels you will meet most often:

nsubj subject: cat in "cat ate" dobj direct object: fish in "ate fish" det determiner: the, a amod adjective: hungry advmod adverb: quickly prep / pobj preposition and its object

Tip: these names follow the style used by spaCy. Other toolkits, such as Universal Dependencies, use slightly different names (for example obj instead of dobj), but the idea is the same.

LEARN: RULES OF THE TREE

Every dependency tree follows three rules

  1. There is exactly one ROOT word.
  2. Every other word has exactly one head.
  3. A head can have many dependents (in the example above, ate has three).

How is it different from a constituency parse?

Constituency
NP, VP, S

Groups words into nested phrases. The tree has phrase labels as extra nodes.

Dependency
nsubj, dobj, det

Links words directly. There are no phrase nodes, only words and labelled arcs.
